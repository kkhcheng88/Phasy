# Vacuum Cupping — Physiotherapy Sources (2026-09-24)

## 1. Summary

Suction-only cupping (static, sliding, and movement/"myofascial decompression") is used in physical therapy and sports rehab clinics as an adjunct, not a standalone cure. Typical dose: 5–10 min per placement (commercial/technique sources say up to 15–20 min max, to avoid blistering), light-to-moderate suction, 1–3x/week with rest days, same-spot sessions spaced by days. Evidence from a 2023 sports-rehab systematic review is low-to-moderate for pain and soft-tissue flexibility, very-low-to-low for other conditions, with a very low adverse-event rate; a 2024 umbrella review is more skeptical, rating overall confidence "low" and effects "short-term." No dedicated professional-body clinical guideline (e.g. APTA/CSP) was found — dosing detail below leans on peer-reviewed studies plus PT-clinic pages and commercial technique guides, marked as such. Avoid list (bony points, spine, major vessels/neck front, lymph nodes, varicose veins, broken skin) is consistent across sources but mostly sourced from general/wellness guides rather than peer-reviewed papers — flagged as lower-tier evidence below.

## 3. Placement: use / avoid, by body area

General avoid-list (cross-checked across sources, mostly general/wellness cupping-safety guides rather than peer-reviewed papers — flagged as lower-tier):
- Bony prominences (spine/vertebral processes, collarbones, kneecaps) — cup will not seal and can bruise/hurt more.
- Directly over the kidney area of the low back.
- Major blood vessels and the front/sides of the neck (carotid area), groin.
- Lymph nodes.
- Varicose veins, moles, broken/inflamed skin, open wounds, rashes.
- Source (lower-tier, general cupping-safety guides, not physio-specific): globalcupping.com "Areas to Never Cup" as reflected in aggregated search summary — https://globalcupping.com/pages/areas-to-never-cup ; general safety compilation — https://wellfr.com/where-should-cups-be-placed-a-guide-to-safe-cupping-therapy-placement

By area (v1 scope: neck, shoulders, upper back, low back, hips):
- **Neck**: use only the back/sides of the neck over trapezius muscle bulk; avoid the front of the neck entirely (airway, carotid artery, jugular vein) — general safety guides above; consistent with the existing IFOMPT vascular-neck-risk framework already logged in `trigger-point-and-treatment-sources.md` (avoid pressure/suction near vascular red flags).
- **Shoulders / upper back**: broad muscular areas (trapezius, rhomboids, lats) are the commonly cited "beginner-safe" sites because they are padded and away from bone; avoid directly over the shoulder blade's bony ridge (scapular spine) and the spine itself. Source: general placement guide — https://wellfr.com/where-should-cups-be-placed-a-guide-to-safe-cupping-therapy-placement
- **Low back**: use the muscle bulk lateral to the spine; avoid the spine itself and the kidney area (roughly the region under the lower ribs, either side of the spine); avoid entirely in pregnancy per contraindications (§4). Sources: kidney-area avoidance — aggregated safety-guide search result (lower-tier, no single peer-reviewed source found) — https://projectxeros.org/cupping-therapy-cup-locations-placement-guide ; pregnancy — see §4 citations.
- **Hips / buttocks**: cited as a common treatment site in a peer-reviewed sports-medicine review (Trofa et al. 2020, alongside back and chest) — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7028774/. Avoid directly over any varicose veins in this region and over any recent surgical scar.

No physiotherapy-specific (as opposed to general/wellness) placement diagram or professional-body placement chart was found. This is a gap — see §7.

## 4. Contraindications, after-effects, warning signs

**Contraindications / precautions** (cross-checked against the cupping-specific systematic reviews already logged in `trigger-point-and-treatment-sources.md`, PMC11955767 and PMC12121573, plus general safety sources below):
- Bleeding disorders (e.g. hemophilia) or anticoagulant medication.
- Pregnancy — avoid abdomen and lower back.
- Active skin infection, inflammation, burns, open wounds, or rash at the site.
- Severe uncontrolled diabetes or diabetic neuropathy at the site, immune suppression, active cancer near the planned site — treat as clinician-only decision, not self-care.
- Elderly with thin/fragile skin — higher risk of skin tearing or severe bruising.
- Connective-tissue disorders (e.g. Ehlers-Danlos) — negative pressure can overstretch vulnerable tissue.
- Fever / acute systemic infection.
- Source: aggregated from — https://fuelhealthwellness.com/cupping-therapy-risks-contraindications/ and https://www.maximumpotentialphysio.ca/cupping-therapy-risks-and-side-effects/ (the second is a physiotherapy-clinic source); cross-checked against cupping systematic reviews — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11955767/ ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12121573/

**Expected after-effects (normal, not a warning sign):**
- Circular discoloration/"cupping marks" resembling a bruise — caused by blood drawn to the skin surface under suction, not a true muscle-fiber bruise. Typically fades within a few days; darker marks can take up to about two weeks. Source: aggregated dermatology/consumer-health summary — https://www.medicinenet.com/cupping/article.htm ; PT-clinic estimate of 2–10 days — Athletico, https://www.athletico.com/services/specialty-techniques/myofascial-decompression-cupping/ ; PT-clinic estimate of "several days to a week" — Confluent Health, https://confluenthealth.com/patients/specialty-services/myofascial-decompression-cupping/

**Warning signs (stop and seek clinical review):**
- Blisters — linked directly to sessions run too long; Trofa et al. 2020 states cupping should not exceed ~20 min to avoid blistering — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7028774/
- Marks that do not fade within about two weeks, or that are accompanied by increasing pain, spreading redness, warmth, or discharge (possible skin infection) — general consumer-health source, no dedicated physio guideline found — https://www.medicinenet.com/cupping/article.htm
- Reported (rare) adverse events in the literature: hematoma, factitial panniculitis, syncope, infection, scarring — already logged with citation in `trigger-point-and-treatment-sources.md` (Korea systematic review) — https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S187638201300084X
- Pain during application beyond mild discomfort — remove the cup; general technique-guide advice, not a clinical study — https://globalcupping.com/blogs/news/cupping-techniques

## 5. Evidence in plain words

- A 2023 systematic review focused specifically on musculoskeletal and sports rehab (22 studies, 1990–2019) found: moderate evidence that cupping improves soft-tissue flexibility; low-to-moderate evidence it reduces low back or neck pain; very-low-to-low evidence for other conditions; adverse events were very rare. In plain words: cupping has some real, but not strong, support for loosening tight tissue and easing back/neck pain, and it appears to carry little safety risk. Source: Mohamed AA, Zhang X, Jan YK. *J Back Musculoskelet Rehabil.* 2023;36(1):3–19. DOI 10.3233/BMR-210242 — https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35848010/ (confirmed via https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/evidence-based-and-adverse-effects-analyses-of-cupping-therapy-in/)
- A 2024 umbrella review (a review of reviews) covering athletes and the general population found overall confidence in the underlying evidence was low, most individual reviews rated cupping's musculoskeletal-pain benefit as not clearly better than doing nothing, and any measured benefit was short-term rather than lasting. In plain words: the higher you go up the evidence pyramid, the weaker cupping's proven benefit looks, and no study followed people long enough to say it produces a lasting fix. Source: ElMeligie MM, Yehia AM, Mohamed MH, Mahmoud M, AlKholui MN, Mohamed GI. *Human Movement.* 2024;25(4):28–43. DOI 10.5114/hm/194774 — https://hummov.awf.wroc.pl/Effectiveness-of-cupping-therapy-for-musculoskeletal-pain-an-umbrella-review,194774,0,2.html
- A sports-medicine review (orthopaedic surgery journal) reports one trial where dry cupping for neck/shoulder pain reduced pain scores by about 6 points on a 10-point scale versus about 0.2–0.6 points in controls, and states cupping's adverse-event profile is favorable when sessions stay under ~20 minutes. In plain words: in at least one study the pain relief was large, but this is a single trial, not the pooled/averaged picture the two reviews above give — treat the 2023/2024 reviews as the more reliable summary. Source: Trofa DP, Obana KK, Herndon CL, et al. *JAAOS Glob Res Rev.* 2020;4(1). DOI 10.5435/JAAOSGlobal-D-19-00105 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7028774/
- Already logged (not repeated in full here, see `trigger-point-and-treatment-sources.md` §3): PMC11955767 and PMC12121573 — both report low/very-low-quality evidence overall, consistent with the two reviews above.

Bottom line for app design: present cupping as "may help some people with muscle tightness and localized pain, evidence is modest and mostly short-term, safety risk is low if dosing/placement/contraindication rules are followed" — not as a guaranteed-effective treatment.

## 7. Open questions

- No physiotherapy-specific placement chart or diagram (professional-body sourced) was found for the neck/shoulders/upper back/low back/hips; the avoid-list above is corroborated across several general/wellness sources but not a single peer-reviewed or professional-body source — worth a follow-up search targeting APTA, CSP (UK Chartered Society of Physiotherapy), or Sports Medicine Australia position statements specifically.
- No numeric suction-strength standard (skin-lift mm or pump-stroke count) exists in the peer-reviewed literature; the app will need to set its own conservative default (e.g. "light lift only, no more than mild discomfort") rather than cite a clinical number.
- No physiotherapy-framed book (only journal articles) was found to fully replace the dropped Chirali TCM book as a narrative reference; if a book format is wanted, further search is needed (unverified there isn't one — absence not confirmed as exhaustive).
- Frequency guidance ("1–3x/week," "not more than once/week per spot") is sourced from commercial cupping-equipment sites, not peer-reviewed dosing studies — flagged lower-tier throughout; treat as a starting default, not an evidence-based limit.
- The umbrella review's own language is internally mixed ("more effective than passive interventions" vs. "generally ineffective") — reported as-is in §5; a full read of the primary text (not just the extracted summary) would be needed to resolve the apparent tension before quoting it verbatim in-app.
